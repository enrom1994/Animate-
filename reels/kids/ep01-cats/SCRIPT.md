## Intro
Hi! I'm Pip, and this is my cat, Biscuit.
Biscuit does four things that are a total mystery.
She purrs. She always lands on her feet. She squishes her paws on my blanket, like she's making bread. And she jumps into every box she can find.
Today, we're going to find out why. Ready? Let's go!

## Mystery 1: The purr
Mystery number one: the purr!
Listen. Biscuit is purring right now. It sounds like a tiny motor.
Can I purr too? I tried. I just sounded like a toy car!
Quick, guess first! Do cats only purr when they're happy? Yes, or no?  *[pause 3 s]*
Most people think a purr means a cat is happy. And lots of the time, it does!
But here's something surprising. Cats also purr when they're scared, or sick, or hurt.
Scientists are still working out exactly why. But they do know how a purr is made.
Deep in Biscuit's throat is her voice box.
When she breathes in and out, two little flaps in there wobble super fast. About twenty five times every second!
That wobbling makes the buzzing sound we call a purr.
A few years ago, scientists found tiny soft pads inside a cat's voice box. They help make that low, rumbly sound.
Mother cats purr to their kittens, and kittens purr back.
Big cats are different. Cheetahs can purr. But lions and tigers can't. They roar instead!
So Biscuit has a little purring engine, right inside her throat. Cool, right?

## Mystery 2: Landing on her feet
Mystery number two: landing on her feet!
Biscuit jumped off the bookshelf this morning, and, plop, she landed on all four paws.
How does she do it?
It's called the righting reflex. Righting means turning the right way up.
First, a part of Biscuit's inner ear tells her which way is up. Her eyes help too.
Guess first! When she turns over, does Biscuit spin all at once, or one half at a time?  *[pause 3 s]*
Then she twists. Her front half turns first, and her back half follows.
She can do that because her back bends really easily, like a spring.
And cats don't have a stiff collarbone like we do, so their shoulders can move a lot.
Then she reaches her legs down and lands softly.
More than a hundred years ago, a scientist filmed a falling cat with a special fast camera.
For the first time, people could see every step, one picture at a time.
Kittens start learning this when they're about three weeks old. By seven weeks, they're experts!
But here's the important part. Cats can still get hurt when they fall.
So we never drop a cat, or let them fall, to see what happens. We keep our cats safe.

## Mystery 3: Making biscuits
Mystery number three: the squishy paws!
Look. Biscuit is pressing her paws up and down on my blanket. Left, right, left, right.
This is called kneading. And people have a funny name for it. They call it making biscuits!
Hey, that's your name, Biscuit!
So why do cats knead?
Guess first! Did it start when they were tiny kittens, or when they were all grown up?  *[pause 3 s]*
It starts when they're tiny kittens.
Baby kittens press their paws on their mom's tummy. It helps the milk come out.
When cats grow up, they keep doing it. Usually, it means they feel safe and happy.
Some cats even close their eyes, purr, or drool a little while they knead. That's one happy cat!
Cats also have scent spots in their paw pads. Scientists think kneading might leave a little bit of their smell behind.
So when Biscuit kneads my blanket, she might be saying: this is my cozy spot.

## Mystery 4: The box
Mystery number four: the box!
Yesterday, a big box came in the mail. And guess who jumped in before I even opened it?
Biscuit! Even when a box is way too small, she squeezes right in.
Guess first! Do cats like boxes because they're bored, or because boxes make them feel safe?  *[pause 3 s]*
Boxes make them feel safe! Inside a box, nobody can sneak up behind you.
Wild cats hide in small, cozy spots too, so they can rest and keep watch.
Scientists tested this at a cat shelter. Cats that got a box to hide in calmed down faster than cats with no box.
Boxes are warm, too. Cardboard keeps the heat in, and cats like it warmer than we do!
And here's the funniest part. Some cats will even sit in a square made of tape on the floor!
Scientists asked cat owners to try this at home. Some cats walked right into the tape square and sat down.
There's no box at all. Just a square. And that's enough!
So if your cat sits in a box, she's not being silly. She's found a safe, warm hideout.

## Outro
So now we know!
Biscuit purrs with a tiny engine in her throat. She lands on her feet by twisting in the air. She makes biscuits because she feels safe. And she loves boxes because they're safe and warm.
Which animal should we find out about next? A dog? A bee? An owl?
Bye for now! Bye, Biscuit!

### Fact notes
- Purr: the larynx vibrates at about 25 Hz; a 2023 study (Herbst et al., Current Biology) found connective-tissue pads in the vocal folds that let them vibrate that low, even without nerve signals. Cats also purr when stressed, ill or hurt; the full purpose is still debated (hence "still working out exactly why").
- Big cats: cheetahs, cougars, lynxes and bobcats purr; lions, tigers, leopards and jaguars roar and do not purr (widely reported; consumer sources, not peer-reviewed).
- Righting reflex: inner-ear balance organ plus eyes sense "up"; a very flexible spine lets the front and back halves rotate in turn; the collarbone is not attached to other bones; develops from about 3 weeks, complete by about 7 weeks; cats can still be injured in falls (Live Science, Purina, PetMD-type veterinary sources).
- Kneading: kittens knead the mother's belly to stimulate milk flow; adults knead when relaxed and content; paw pads have scent glands and kneading *may* leave scent (Scientific American quoting an Oklahoma State behaviour scientist; PetMD; Chewy). Hedged in the script.
- Safety line in chapter 2 is deliberate: children may otherwise try dropping a cat.
- Box, shelter study: Vinke, Godijn & van der Leij (2014), Applied Animal Behaviour Science: shelter cats given a hiding box had lower stress scores in the first days than cats without one ("calmed down faster"; by two weeks the groups were similar).
- Box, warmth: cats' comfortable temperature range (thermoneutral zone) is roughly 30-38 C, warmer than ours (often cited from the US National Research Council, 2006); cardboard insulates.
- Tape squares: Smith, Chan & Byosiere (2021), "If I fits I sits", Applied Animal Behaviour Science, a citizen-science study where owners laid tape outlines at home. Only some cats took part fully and sat in a shape, hence "some cats", not "most".
- Falling cat film: Etienne-Jules Marey's 1894 chronophotographs of a falling cat (published in Comptes rendus and Nature).
- Kneading extras (eyes closed, purring, drooling while kneading): common veterinary-behaviour descriptions of relaxed kneading.
- Wild cats resting in dens/crevices: general natural history, phrased loosely ("small, cozy spots").
