# The Kansas City Massacre (long form, 16:9)

## 1 · Cold open
*(no card; ends on a title card: THE KANSAS CITY MASSACRE)*

At seven fifteen on a Saturday morning in June 1933, a train pulled into Union Station in Kansas City.
On board were three lawmen and one prisoner.
Minutes later, five men lay dead beside a parked car.
The newspapers called it the Union Station Massacre.
It made a famous outlaw the most hunted man in America.
And it changed federal law enforcement for good.
But to understand that morning, you have to start with the prisoner.
A man who walked out of one of America's toughest prisons, and simply never came back.

## 2 · PART ONE: THE TRUSTY (Leavenworth · 1924 to 1930)

His name was Frank Nash.
In 1924, he was sentenced to twenty five years in the federal penitentiary at Leavenworth, for mail robbery.
Inside, he played the model prisoner.
By 1930 he was cooking for the deputy warden, a trusted inmate with privileges.
On October 19, 1930, he was sent outside the walls on an errand.
He never came back.
For almost three years, Frank Nash lived as a free man.
He moved through Chicago and other big cities, and kept company with the underworld.
In December 1931, he helped free seven prisoners from Fort Leavenworth.
And he fell in love with a barmaid named Frances Luce.
In May 1933, he married her, under a false name.

## 3 · PART TWO: HOT SPRINGS (Arkansas · June 1933)

Hot Springs, Arkansas, was a resort town, famous for its baths, its illegal casinos, and its tolerance for men like Nash.
He was often seen at the White Front Cigar Store, owned by a man named Richard Galatas.
In June 1933, two federal agents, Joe Lackey and Frank Smith, found out where he was.
But in 1933, federal agents had a problem.
By law, they had no general power to make arrests, or to carry guns.
So they brought a local lawman with them: Otto Reed, the police chief of McAlester, Oklahoma.
On June 16, they found Nash, and took him.
They didn't stay in Hot Springs.
They drove straight to Fort Smith, Arkansas, and the railroad.

## 4 · PART THREE: THE WORD GETS OUT (Fort Smith to Kansas City · June 16, 1933)

News of the arrest travelled faster than the lawmen.
Nash's friends in Hot Springs learned that he had been taken, and where he was headed.
That night, the three lawmen boarded a Missouri Pacific train with their prisoner, bound for Kansas City.
From there, the plan was to drive him the last stretch, back to Leavenworth.
Somewhere ahead of them, a different plan was being made.
Kansas City in 1933 was a city where gangsters operated with little fear of the police.
Investigators later said Nash's friends turned to a hired gun there named Vernon Miller, a former South Dakota sheriff turned killer.
Miller agreed to get Nash back.

## 5 · PART FOUR: UNION STATION (Kansas City · 7:15 a.m.)

The train arrived at about seven fifteen.
Waiting on the platform were two more federal agents, Reed Vetterli and Raymond Caffrey, and two Kansas City detectives, W. J. Grooms and Frank Hermanson.
The group walked Nash through the station, out to Caffrey's Chevrolet, parked by the east entrance.
Nash was put in the front seat.
Lackey, Smith and Chief Reed climbed into the back.
Caffrey walked around to the driver's door.
He never got in.
Two armed men ran out from behind a parked car.
One shouted, "Up, up!"
Then someone yelled, "Let 'em have it."

## 6 · PART FIVE: THIRTY SECONDS (The east entrance)

The shooting lasted about thirty seconds.
Grooms and Hermanson died where they stood.
Agent Caffrey was shot in the head.
Inside the car, Chief Reed was killed. So was Frank Nash, the man the gunmen had come to free.
Agent Lackey was hit three times in the back. Vetterli was wounded in the arm.
Only Agent Smith came through unhurt.
Then a voice called out: "They're all dead. Let's get out of here."
A Kansas City officer fired at one of the gunmen as he ran. The men escaped in a dark car.
By that evening, the story was on front pages across the country.

## 7 · PART SIX: THE HUNT (1933 to 1938)

The Bureau named three suspects.
Vernon Miller, the hired gun. Adam Richetti. And the most famous outlaw in the Midwest, Charles "Pretty Boy" Floyd.
Miller never stood trial. In November 1933, his body was found in a ditch outside Detroit.
Floyd was shot dead on an Ohio farm in October 1934. With his last breath, he reportedly denied any part in the massacre.
Richetti was captured in Ohio that same month. He was convicted, and in 1938 he died in Missouri's gas chamber.
He was the only man ever convicted for the killings.
To this day, historians argue over whether Floyd and Richetti were there at all.
One writer, Robert Unger, went further. He argued that some of the fatal shots came from a lawman's own shotgun, fired in panic.
The official account still blames the gunmen.

## 8 · PART SEVEN: WHAT IT CHANGED (1934 and after)

The massacre shocked the country.
Four lawmen had died escorting one prisoner, and the federal agents with them had no clear legal power to carry guns.
In 1934, Congress changed that. Federal agents could now carry firearms and make arrests.
Their Bureau soon got a new name: the Federal Bureau of Investigation.
Many historians see that morning at Union Station as the moment the modern FBI was born.
Union Station still stands in Kansas City.
And near the east entrance, people still point out marks in the stone they say were left by the bullets.
Four lawmen. One prisoner. Thirty seconds.

### Fact notes
- Nash: 25-year sentence at Leavenworth on March 1, 1924; walked out on an errand on October 19, 1930; helped free seven prisoners from Fort Leavenworth in December 1931; married Frances Luce on May 26, 1933 under the name "Moore" (Wikipedia, Frank Nash).
- Capture: agents Lackey and Smith with Chief Otto Reed, because agents were then barred from carrying weapons and making arrests; arrested June 16 and driven to Fort Smith; Missouri Pacific train that night (Wikipedia, Frank Nash).
- Union Station: met by Vetterli, Caffrey, Grooms and Hermanson; Caffrey's Chevrolet by the east entrance; Nash in front, Lackey, Smith and Reed in the back; "Up, up!", "Let 'em have it!", about 30 seconds, "They're all dead. Let's get out of here." (Wikipedia, Kansas City massacre).
- Fates: Miller found dead near Detroit on November 29, 1933; Floyd killed on October 22, 1934 near Clarkson, Ohio; Richetti convicted in 1935 and executed on October 7, 1938 (Wikipedia).
- Contested, and kept hedged: Floyd and Richetti's involvement (several authors say they were framed); Unger's argument (The Union Station Massacre, 1997) that a lawman's shotgun fire killed some victims; the bullet marks on the station (a local claim).
- Not yet verified, so check or cut before release: Miller as "a former South Dakota sheriff". It is widely reported, but I haven't confirmed it against a primary source.
- Sources for the description: Wikipedia, "Kansas City massacre" and "Frank Nash"; FBI, "Kansas City Massacre / Pretty Boy Floyd" (famous cases); Robert Unger, The Union Station Massacre (1997).
